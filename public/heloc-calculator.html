HELOC Calculator: How Much Can I Borrow? (2026 Guide)

Updated May 2026 · 8 min read · myloanbreakdown.com

A Home Equity Line of Credit — or HELOC — is one of the most flexible financial tools available to homeowners. You borrow against your home's equity, repay it, and borrow again — like a credit card, but at a fraction of the interest rate. But how much can you actually get, and is a HELOC right for you?

This guide covers everything you need to know: how lenders calculate your credit limit, what the 85% CLTV rule means in dollars, current HELOC rates, how the draw and repayment periods work, and what you need to qualify in 2026.

How Much Can You Borrow with a HELOC?

HELOC credit limits are based on your Combined Loan-to-Value (CLTV) ratio — the percentage of your home's value that all loans combined represent. Most lenders cap HELOCs at 85% CLTV, though some go to 90% for well-qualified borrowers.

HELOC Maximum Formula Maximum HELOC = (Home Value × 85%) − Current Mortgage Balance

📊 Example Calculation

Home Value$500,000
× 85% CLTV limit$425,000
− Current Mortgage Balance$290,000
= Maximum HELOC Credit Line$135,000

On a $500,000 home with a $290,000 mortgage, at 85% CLTV you could access a HELOC credit line of up to $135,000. You don't need to draw all of it — you can take $20,000 now, repay it, and draw again later.

How a HELOC Works: Draw Period vs. Repayment Period

A HELOC has two distinct phases — and the payment difference between them catches many borrowers off guard.

Draw Period (typically 5–10 years)

During the draw period, you can borrow from and repay the line freely. Most HELOCs require interest-only payments during this phase — you pay interest on what you've drawn, not the full credit limit.

Example: $50,000 HELOC at 9.0%, draw period payment = $50,000 × 9% ÷ 12 = $375/month interest only

Repayment Period (typically 10–20 years)

When the draw period ends, the line closes and you can no longer borrow. The outstanding balance converts to a fully amortizing loan — meaning you now pay principal + interest. The payment jump can be significant.

Same example: $50,000 balance, 9.0%, 20-year repayment = $449/month — a $74/month increase

⚠️ Payment Shock Warning If you've drawn heavily on your HELOC during the draw period, the payment increase at repayment can be significant. Our HELOC calculator shows you both payments side by side so you can plan ahead — not be surprised.

The Prime Rate is currently 8.50% (May 2026). If the Federal Reserve raises rates, your HELOC rate goes up automatically — and so does your payment. This is the core risk of a HELOC vs. a fixed-rate home equity loan.

💡 Rate Cap Protection Most HELOCs have a lifetime rate cap — typically 18% — that limits how high the rate can go. Ask your lender for the lifetime cap AND the periodic cap (how much it can increase per adjustment period). These are negotiable at some lenders.

What You Need to Qualify for a HELOC in 2026

Credit Score

Most lenders require a minimum 620 credit score for a HELOC, but you'll get meaningfully better rates at 720+. For the maximum credit line at the best rate, aim for 740+.

Equity Position

You need at least 15% equity remaining after the HELOC (to stay under 85% CLTV). Most lenders also want you to have been on title for at least 6–12 months before applying.

Debt-to-Income Ratio

Most HELOC lenders cap total DTI at 43%. This includes your existing mortgage payment plus the new HELOC payment plus all other monthly debts. Note that lenders often qualify you at the fully drawn amount — not just what you plan to draw — so even an unused credit line affects your DTI qualification.

Stable Income

Unlike DSCR loans, HELOCs require income verification. Most lenders want 2 years of W-2s, tax returns, or bank statements for self-employed borrowers. Consistent, documentable income is key.

Property Type

Primary residences get the best terms. Second homes and investment properties face lower CLTV limits and higher rates — and not all lenders offer HELOCs on non-primary properties.

Credit unions typically offer the lowest HELOC fees and most competitive rates — especially for members. Check your local credit union before going to a big bank.

HELOC Tax Deductibility

HELOC interest may be tax deductible — but only if the funds are used to buy, build, or substantially improve the home securing the loan. Using HELOC funds for a home renovation = potentially deductible. Using them to pay off credit cards or take a vacation = not deductible. Consult a tax professional for your specific situation.

When a HELOC Makes Sense

  • Home renovation in phases — draw for each phase, repay between phases
  • Emergency fund backup — open the line now, only pay if you draw
  • Business cash flow — flexible access for seasonal businesses
  • Bridge financing — access equity before selling your current home
  • Tuition payments — draw each semester rather than taking the full amount upfront

When a HELOC Doesn't Make Sense

  • You need a fixed, predictable payment — choose a home equity loan instead
  • You're on a tight budget — rate increases could strain your payment
  • Your current mortgage rate is low — a HELOC avoids touching it, which is good, but the HELOC rate itself may be high
  • You want to consolidate debt long-term — a fixed home equity loan gives more certainty
✅ Pro Tip: Open It Before You Need It HELOCs are much easier to get when you don't urgently need money. If you're employed and have good credit, consider opening a HELOC now as an emergency backstop — you pay nothing until you draw, and having the line available can save you in a financial pinch. You can always close it if you never need it.
